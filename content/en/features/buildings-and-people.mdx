Supply chains feed a city; the needs ladder gives it a direction. In Beyond These Stars, your Pequeninos climb from survival toward purpose, and the buildings you place are the rungs they climb on. This page maps the ladder: what citizens need at each stage, which buildings serve which needs, the order that works in practice, and the quietly-missed needs that turn a fed city into an unhappy one. It pairs with the supply chain guide — chains are the supply side of the economy, people are the demand side, and the mayor's job is keeping both honest.

## The Ladder, Bottom to Top

Citizen needs arrive in a rough order, and honoring the order is most of the game's social system. **Survival first**: beds, food, warmth — the needs that exist from the first landing, served by the beginner guide's earliest loops. **Security and stability next**: reliable supply, safe housing, the sense that tomorrow's meals exist — served less by a building than by your loops running consistently; a city that repeats famine-and-recovery cycles carries an unhappiness no amenity erases. **Then work and belonging**: Pequeninos want roles — jobs that matter, workplaces they can reach, the dignity of contribution — and this is where placement decisions made for logistics start paying social dividends, because a worker whose job is a short walk away is a satisfied worker twice over. **Then education**: the schools that grow the population into the specialists deeper chains demand, and the rung where cities either invest early or stall late. **And toward the top, purpose**: the needs the journey's story eventually makes explicit — the satisfactions of a civilization that knows why it travels between the stars, served by the late-game cultural buildings and the chapters that unlock them.

The ladder's operating rule: **satisfy the lowest unsatisfied rung first.** It sounds obvious; it is violated constantly, because the higher rungs have prettier buildings. The unhappy-city diagnostic in most sessions is a mayor building purpose-tier amenities over an education shortfall — a city with an opera house and unstaffed schools is a genre joke that Beyond These Stars lets you actually build, to your regret.

## Buildings and What They Serve

The building catalog, organized by the need it serves rather than the menu it appears in. **Housing** — beds first, better beds as materials allow; the placement law from every guide applies doubly (housing walks to everything, so cluster it with the work it serves). **Food chain buildings** — farms, foragers, kitchens and stores; the survival rung's whole world. **Production buildings** — the woodcutters, quarries and processors of the supply chains; social notes apply (workers housed near work are happy workers). **Schools** — the education rung's engine; place them inside existing districts where the citizens they serve already live, not in the expansion that "will need it soon." **Amenities and cultural buildings** — the happiness and purpose tiers; their catchment matters more than their count, because an amenity no one can reach is a decoration with a maintenance cost.

The general placement law for social buildings: **serve the walk, not the map.** A school "central" on the map but a ridge-crossing away from its students serves no one; a school tucked into the housing cluster serves everyone it should. The curved-map lessons of the supply guide apply unchanged — the whale's geometry decides what central means, district by district.

## The Needs New Mayors Miss

The quiet failures, named. **The educated bottleneck**: chains deepen faster than schools graduate specialists, and the city builds advanced buildings no one qualified can staff — the fix is starting education the moment stability allows, not when the shortfall arrives. **The reachability gap**: amenities placed by aesthetics — the beautiful hilltop amphitheater — that sit outside the walking network of the people they exist for; the fix is placing social buildings where the paths already are. **The overwork city**: production scaled past what the population can humanly staff, leaving needs unmet on both ends — workers too busy for the amenities that would satisfy them, amenities unstaffed for want of the workers too busy to enjoy them; the fix is population growth before production growth, schools before factories. **And the monotony trap**: districts built as pure housing or pure industry, which the Pequeninos experience as places to sleep or places to work but not places to live; the fix is mixed districts at human scale, which — pleasingly — is also the efficient shape, because mixed districts shorten every walk.

## Why the Ladder Is the Game's Conscience

What the needs ladder does for Beyond These Stars is give the logistics a meaning. A supply chain in the abstract optimizes for throughput; a supply chain feeding a ladder of evolving human needs optimizes for something warmer — a city that takes care of its people as they grow into what the journey asks of them. The game's gentle tone lives here, in systems that reward care without punishing experimentation. The closing placement advice mirrors the complete guide's: build for the Pequeninos you have, house them near their work, teach them before you need them, and the whaleback grows a civilization that reads as cared-for from orbit. That is the whole social game — a ladder climbed together, at walking speed, on the back of a whale.
